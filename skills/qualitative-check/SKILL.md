---
name: qualitative-check
description: Check a qualitative study against SRQR or COREQ, covering the researchers, the sampling, data collection and analysis.
---

Use this for the report of qualitative research: interviews, focus groups, observation, document analysis, or the qualitative part of a mixed-methods study.

## Get the checklist

1. Establish which guideline the journal asks for: COREQ covers interviews and focus groups, and SRQR covers qualitative research of any approach. A synthesis of qualitative studies has its own guideline (ENTREQ) and the points below do not fit it: use `checklist-check` for that. If the journal names none, propose the one that fits the methods and confirm with the collaborator. Obtain its official checklist: a file the collaborator adds to the project, or fetched with fetch_url from the EQUATOR Network (www.equator-network.org). Do not recite items from memory. If the fetch does not return a usable checklist (many are PDF or Word downloads), ask the collaborator to add the file to the project; do not go on from memory.
2. Ask whether the journal has its own form or wants the completed checklist as a separate file.
3. When an item's meaning is unclear, read the guideline's own paper for how it defines the item, and ask the collaborator if it stays unclear.

## Read

4. Read the whole manuscript, with its tables, any interview or topic guide, and the supplementary files in the project.

## Assess

5. For each item, in the checklist's order, record: reported, partly reported, not reported, or not applicable, with the location (section and paragraph, or page and line if the form asks for them) and a brief quotation or the table or figure that supports the judgment. "Partly reported" names what is missing; "not applicable" gives the reason.
6. Judge reporting, not conduct: an item is reported when the manuscript says what was done, including that something was not done. Do not infer a step from usual practice.
7. Pay particular attention to:
   - the qualitative approach or methodology is named with its rationale, and the methods used are consistent with it;
   - the researchers: who collected and analysed the data, their background and relationship with participants, and how this may have shaped the work;
   - the setting and context, and why it was chosen;
   - sampling: the strategy, how participants were approached, how many took part, how many declined or withdrew and why, and the stated basis for stopping (if saturation is claimed, how it was judged);
   - data collection: who, where, how long, with what guide, whether recorded and transcribed, field notes, and whether anyone else was present;
   - analysis: how codes and themes were derived, by how many people, with what software, and what was done to support trustworthiness (for example member checking, triangulation, an audit trail) as reported, not as assumed;
   - findings: each theme is supported by data, quotations are attributed to a participant identifier, and the participant numbers in the text agree with the tables;
   - ethics approval and consent, and how confidentiality was protected. Flag quotations or descriptions that could identify a participant in a small setting;
   - do not judge the study by quantitative standards the guideline does not apply, such as sample size calculations or statistical generalisation.

## Report

8. Produce the completed checklist as a table in the form the journal asks for, with the official item wording and order, then a short list of gaps, most important first, with the information needed for each, and separately any inconsistency found in step 7.
9. Suggest wording only from what the manuscript or the collaborator provides. Never invent study details, participant numbers, quotations, or statements about reflexivity, saturation or approval; ask for them and leave a marked placeholder.
10. Edit the manuscript only when asked, and update the locations in the checklist afterwards, since page and line numbers move.
